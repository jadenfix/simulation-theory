\begin{abstract}
Arguments about whether an observer is simulated often move quickly from a claim about the abundance of simulated observers to a posterior probability of being simulated. This paper isolates three prior questions that must be answered before that update is well-defined: whether the competing hypotheses induce different observable laws, whether the observer measure is invariant to representational refinements that change no observable process, and whether repeated evidence corresponds to repeated draws from the population-level latent variable whose prevalence is being inferred. We give elementary but operationally sharp results for each question. First, observationally equivalent hypotheses have Bayes factor one for every internal transcript. Second, splitting one latent component into arbitrarily many positive-weight observational clones preserves every finite shared-latent observation law while arbitrary label counting can move the apparent category probability arbitrarily close to zero or one. On rational weights, normalized local split-additivity uniquely recovers probability weight. Third, when one latent model is drawn once and an arbitrarily long transcript is generated conditional on it, the likelihood ratio between two hypotheses that differ only in their mixing weights is a posterior-weighted convex combination of the component weight ratios; within-world repetition therefore does not become repeated sampling from the hyperprior. We then distinguish conditional identifiability of mixture weights given an observation channel from joint identifiability of the prior and channel, exhibit a continuous one-view factorization gauge, and show that within this gauge two conditionally independent views sharing the same persistent latent variable collapse the continuous ambiguity to label permutation under explicit rank and positivity assumptions. The result is not an argument that reality is or is not simulated. It is a set of identifiability and invariance conditions that any quantitative anthropic simulation argument must satisfy before its numerical posterior can be interpreted as evidence rather than as a consequence of representation, reference-class, or sampling choices.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Bostrom's simulation argument is deliberately a trilemma rather than a direct empirical proof that we inhabit a simulation \citep{bostrom2003}. Subsequent discussion has focused heavily on the indifference principle, reference classes, self-location, and what evidence could support a high simulant-to-non-simulant ratio \citep{weatherson2003,crawford2013,franceschi2014,richmond2017,thomas2024}. The central issue in this paper is logically prior to many disagreements in that literature.

Before asking how a Bayesian agent should update on being an observer, we should ask what statistical experiment the agent is participating in. What exactly was sampled? Which latent variable persists through time? Which variables are redrawn? What is observable? Which distinctions are merely different descriptions of the same observable process? And does the proposed measure change if we duplicate a latent label without changing anything that can happen?

These questions are mundane in statistics. A mixture parameter is not estimable before it is identifiable; a repeated conditional observation is not automatically a repeated sample from a population mixing distribution; a latent parameterization is not physical evidence merely because it contains more labels. The same discipline is useful in anthropic reasoning.

Our contribution is a compact hierarchy of necessary conditions for quantitative simulation-style Bayes. The individual ingredients connect to established literatures: observation selection and reference classes \citep{bostrom2002}; objections to indifference in the simulation argument \citep{weatherson2003}; reference-class sensitivity \citep{franceschi2014,richmond2017}; recent Bayesian reconstructions of simulation arguments \citep{thomas2024}; branching indifference and branch counting in Everettian probability \citep{wilson2013,dizadji2015,khawaja2026}; and identifiability of finite latent-variable models \citep{allman2009}. We do not claim priority for those general literatures. The contribution is to put observable equivalence, refinement invariance, hierarchical sampling semantics, and latent-factorization identifiability into one auditable framework aimed specifically at simulation-style observer arguments.

The paper makes five points. First, observational equivalence blocks internal discrimination. Second, raw latent-label counting is representation-sensitive. Third, refinement additivity forces ordinary rational probability weight within a narrow local weight-only class. Fourth, repeated observations generated after one persistent latent draw are not repeated samples from the population mixing distribution. Fifth, when both a latent prior and its observation channel are free, one-view factorization can be nonidentifiable; additional shared-latent views can remove a specific continuous gauge under explicit assumptions.

\section{Observable equivalence comes first}
Let $H\in\{B,S\}$ denote two hypotheses, informally ``base'' and ``simulated.'' Let $Y$ denote the complete transcript available to an internal observer under a declared observation and intervention protocol. Write $P_B$ and $P_S$ for the induced probability laws on transcripts.

\begin{theorem}[Observable-equivalence ceiling]\label{thm:equiv}
If $P_B=P_S$ as probability measures on the allowed transcript space, then every measurable statistic of $Y$ has the same law under both hypotheses. For every transcript $y$ with positive probability under the common law,
\[
\mathrm{BF}_{S:B}(y)=\frac{P_S(y)}{P_B(y)}=1.
\]
Consequently no internal decision rule has classification accuracy above chance under equal prior odds solely from $Y$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Equality of measures is preserved by measurable pushforward. The likelihood ratio is one on common positive support. Under equal priors, optimal binary classification accuracy is $(1+\operatorname{TV}(P_B,P_S))/2=1/2$ because total variation is zero.
\end{proof}

This theorem does not say simulation hypotheses are inherently untestable. A restricted simulator architecture may predict a different observable law. It says that an implementation distinction cannot be inferred from observations after the hypotheses have been stipulated to induce the same distribution on every allowed observation and intervention.

\section{Representation invariance and the counting problem}
Reference classes are often introduced as sets of observers or observer-moments \citep{bostrom2002}. Different choices can alter simulation-argument conclusions \citep{franceschi2014,richmond2017}. A separate issue is whether a probability changes under an arbitrary refinement of the representation even when the underlying stochastic process does not.

Consider a finite latent model $M\in\{1,\ldots,m\}$ with weights $w_i>0$, $\sum_iw_i=1$, and conditional transcript laws $K_i$. Its observable law is $P(Y\in A)=\sum_iw_iK_i(A)$.

\begin{definition}[Observational refinement]
A component $(w,K)$ is refined into $r$ clones $(w_1,K),\ldots,(w_r,K)$ when every $w_j>0$ and $\sum_jw_j=w$. The clones have identical conditional observable laws.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Finite shared-latent refinement invariance]\label{thm:clone}
Replacing any component by observational clones preserves the one-view observable law and every finite conditionally independent shared-latent view law.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For one view, the refined contribution is $\sum_jw_jK=wK$. For $T$ conditionally independent views sharing the same latent component, the parent contribution is $wK^{\otimes T}$ and the refined contribution is $\sum_jw_jK^{\otimes T}=wK^{\otimes T}$.
\end{proof}

Suppose initially there is one base label and one simulated label, each with probability weight $1/2$. Uniform label counting assigns simulated mass $1/2$. Refine only the simulated label into $r$ positive-weight observational clones. The observable law is unchanged, but uniform label counting becomes $r/(r+1)\to1$. Refine only the base label and the same convention becomes $1/(r+1)\to0$. The example is intentionally simple: it separates physical or probabilistic multiplicity from the number of names used in a latent representation.

This issue has a useful analogue in Everettian probability. Branching indifference has been defended as a condition on rational probability \citep{wilson2013}, while branch-counting alternatives and the status of branch number remain contested \citep{dizadji2015,khawaja2026}. Our claim is not that anthropic observer measures are Everettian branch measures. It is that both problems expose the need to justify why a proposed count is invariant under transformations regarded as physically or observationally irrelevant.

\subsection{A narrow uniqueness result}
\begin{theorem}[Rational refinement-additive uniqueness]\label{thm:add}
Let $\mu:[0,1]\cap\mathbb Q\to\mathbb R$ satisfy $\mu(1)=1$ and finite split additivity: whenever $w=\sum_{j=1}^rw_j$ for nonnegative rational weights,
\[
\mu(w)=\sum_{j=1}^r\mu(w_j).
\]
Then $\mu(w)=w$ for every rational $w\in[0,1]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Splitting $1$ into $n$ equal pieces gives $n\mu(1/n)=1$. Hence $\mu(1/n)=1/n$. Summing $p$ such pieces gives $\mu(p/n)=p/n$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is elementary and we make no novelty claim for rational additivity. Its role is to force an assumption into view. A measure that depends only on already-declared probability weight and is invariant under every rational refinement has no freedom to substitute raw label multiplicity for that weight. A measure using consciousness, computation, causal depth, spacetime volume, or another physical variable may behave differently, but then that variable is part of the model rather than a consequence of counting syntax.

\section{Persistent latents and effective sample size}
Population-level uncertainty about which kind of world is selected is different from uncertainty about observations generated inside one selected world.

Let a persistent latent model $M$ be drawn once. Conditional on $M=m$, an arbitrary transcript $Y$ has law $P_m$. Consider two hypotheses $H_a,H_b$ that share the conditional laws $P_m$ but assign different mixing weights $a_m$ and $b_m$.

\begin{theorem}[Persistent-latent likelihood-ratio ceiling]\label{thm:persist}
For a transcript $y$, assume $b_m>0$ for every component with $P_m(y)>0$. Then
\[
\frac{P_a(y)}{P_b(y)}
=\sum_m P_b(M=m\mid y)\frac{a_m}{b_m},
\]
and therefore
\[
\min_m\frac{a_m}{b_m}\le\frac{P_a(y)}{P_b(y)}\le\max_m\frac{a_m}{b_m}
\]
over components with positive posterior weight.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Expand $P_a(y)=\sum_ma_mP_m(y)$ and $P_b(y)=\sum_mb_mP_m(y)$, multiply and divide each summand by $P_b(y)$, and recognize $b_mP_m(y)/P_b(y)$ as the posterior probability of component $m$ under $H_b$. The likelihood ratio is therefore a convex combination of the component ratios.
\end{proof}

The support assumption matters. If $b_m=0$ and $a_m>0$ on a component capable of producing $y$, the likelihood ratio can be infinite.

\begin{example}[One world observed one hundred times]
Let $M\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(\theta)$ be drawn once and let $Y_t=M$ noiselessly for every $t$. Then $P_\theta(Y_1=\cdots=Y_T=1)=\theta$ for every $T\ge1$, not $\theta^T$. Comparing $\theta_a=3/4$ and $\theta_b=1/4$ gives Bayes factor $3$ after one observed $1$ and still $3$ after one hundred repeated $1$s. The factor $3^{100}$ describes a different causal experiment in which a fresh $M_t$ is independently redrawn before every observation.
\end{example}

The distinction is easy to state and easy to violate. A long transcript may make the active component almost certain. It does not thereby create many independent observations of the hyperprior from which that component was selected.

\section{Latent prior/channel factorization}
Let $K$ be an $m\times n$ row-stochastic observation channel and $\pi$ a latent prior. A one-view observed law is $q=\pi K$.

\subsection{Known channel}
When $K$ is known, $\pi$ is identifiable from $q$ iff the rows of $K$ are affinely independent. Choosing row $m$ as reference, this is equivalent to rank $m-1$ of the row-difference matrix. Rank failure yields a nonzero zero-sum direction $\delta$ with $\delta K=0$, so nearby distinct interior priors induce the same observed law. This is elementary finite-mixture geometry; much broader identifiability theory for latent structure models uses rank and tensor arguments \citep{allman2009}.

\subsection{Unknown channel}
\begin{proposition}[Row-stochastic factorization gauge]\label{prop:gauge}
Let $A$ be invertible and row-stochastic. Set $K'=AK$ and $\pi'=\pi A^{-1}$. Whenever $\pi'$ is a valid prior,
\[
\pi'K'=\pi K.
\]
Thus distinct prior/channel pairs can be exactly one-view observationally equivalent.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$(\pi A^{-1})(AK)=\pi K$. Nonnegative row-stochastic $A$ maps probability rows to probability rows, so $K'$ remains a channel.
\end{proof}

For an interior prior, sufficiently small near-identity stochastic transformations can produce a continuum of valid equivalent factorizations. Infinite one-view data can identify $q$ while leaving $(\pi,K)$ structurally underdetermined.

\section{Shared-latent views can change the experiment}
Suppose two observations are conditionally independent given the same persistent latent component. Their joint law is
\[
T=K^\top D_\pi K,
\]
where $D_\pi=\operatorname{diag}(\pi)$.

The following result is intentionally scoped to the gauge in Proposition~\ref{prop:gauge}. It is not an unrestricted theorem that two views identify all finite latent-class models.

\begin{theorem}[Two-view rigidity inside the row-stochastic gauge]\label{thm:twoview}
Assume $K$ has full row rank, $A$ is invertible, nonnegative and row-stochastic, $K'=AK$, $\pi'=\pi A^{-1}$ is strictly positive, and both the one-view law and the shared-latent two-view law are preserved. Then $A$ is a permutation matrix.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Preservation of the two-view law gives
\[
K^\top A^\top D_{\pi'}AK=K^\top D_\pi K.
\]
Full row rank of $K$ implies the existence of a right inverse $R$ with $KR=I_m$. Multiplying by $R^\top$ on the left and $R$ on the right yields
\[
A^\top D_{\pi'}A=D_\pi.
\]
For distinct columns $j\ne k$, the $(j,k)$ entry is zero:
\[
0=\sum_i\pi_i'A_{ij}A_{ik}.
\]
Every term is nonnegative and every $\pi_i'$ is strictly positive, so $A_{ij}A_{ik}=0$ for every row $i$ and every $j\ne k$. Hence each row contains at most one positive entry. Row stochasticity forces exactly one entry equal to one. Invertibility forces distinct rows to select distinct columns. Therefore $A$ is a permutation matrix.
\end{proof}

The theorem illustrates why ``more observations'' is too coarse a phrase. If two observations arise after independent redraws of the latent label, their joint law is simply $q\otimes q$ and does not expose the same shared-latent second moment. What matters is the causal coupling between observations.

\section{Implications for simulation arguments}
The preceding results suggest a checklist that comes before a numerical anthropic posterior.

\paragraph{1. Specify the observational hypothesis.} ``Simulated'' is not itself a likelihood. A restricted simulator hypothesis must specify an observable or interventional law.
